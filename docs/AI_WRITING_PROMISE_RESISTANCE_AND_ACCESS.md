# AI Writing: Promise, Resistance and Access

*A balanced report on authorship, productivity, education, creative labour, and disability inclusion*

**Prepared 31 July 2026**

*Central finding: AI writing is most defensible when it reduces barriers while leaving purpose, evidence, voice and final responsibility with the human writer.*

## Executive summary

Debate about AI writing is often distorted by treating every use as equivalent. A spelling correction, a speech-to-text clean-up, an outline, a rewritten paragraph and an entirely generated assignment all involve different transfers of control. The ethically important questions are what the system did, what the human still did, what the audience was entitled to expect, and who remains accountable for the result.

- **The case for limits.** Opposition is strongest where AI obscures authorship, weakens the validity of an assessment, fabricates facts or citations, standardises voice, uses private or copyrighted material without acceptable safeguards, or reduces paid human work and credit. These are substantive concerns, not merely fear of a new tool.

- **The case for benefits.** On bounded professional writing tasks, controlled research has found large immediate gains. In one experiment with 453 professionals, access to ChatGPT reduced completion time by 40 per cent and increased evaluator-rated quality by 18 per cent, with larger gains for lower-performing participants (Noy & Zhang, 2023). Other workplace evidence also suggests that less-experienced workers can benefit disproportionately (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025).

- **The access case.** For neurodivergent and disabled people, AI can operate as an access layer: organising an ADHD user's idea dump, explaining tone to an autistic writer, reducing spelling and decoding demands for a dyslexic writer, expanding abbreviations for an AAC user, or conserving the limited energy of someone living with pain or fatigue. Disability-specific studies report benefits in planning, expression, autonomy and reduced effort, while also documenting inaccessible interfaces, accuracy burdens, subscription costs and pressure to conform to a narrow 'professional' voice (Pierrès et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025).

- **The practical conclusion.** Policies should protect low-risk assistive uses, allow substantive co-writing when it is disclosed and verified, and restrict deceptive substitution, unsafe handling of confidential data, and use in assessments explicitly designed to measure unaided skill. Blanket bans can unintentionally remove accommodations; blanket permission can erase authorship and learning goals.

**A useful default:** use AI to remove friction and widen options, not to conceal responsibility. The human writer should be able to explain, verify and stand behind the final text.

## Introduction: writing is both a product and a practice

Writing produces an artefact – an email, essay, report, story or application – but it is also a way of thinking. Drafting forces a writer to select evidence, test relationships, recognise uncertainty and decide how to address another person. This dual role explains why AI writing can be simultaneously useful and unsettling. It may improve the product while bypassing part of the practice. In a workplace that may be efficient; in a classroom designed to develop reasoning, the same delegation may defeat the task.

The term 'AI writing' usually refers to generative systems, particularly large language models, that predict and produce text in response to instructions. Yet the boundary between generative AI and older assistive technology is porous. Autocomplete, grammar checking, translation, dictation, sentence prediction and text simplification can all contain AI components. A fair analysis therefore begins with function and context rather than the mere presence of AI.

This report is a narrative evidence review rather than a systematic review. It was prepared with generative-AI assistance, and its cited sources were checked against publisher or institutional records. The research base is developing rapidly, is concentrated in English-language and short-term studies, and is particularly limited for many disability groups. Findings should therefore be read as evidence about particular tasks and users, not as universal effects of all tools.

## 1. AI writing is a spectrum, not a single act

The most productive distinction is between assistance that removes a barrier, assistance that develops a writer's work, collaboration that contributes expression, and substitution that supplies the work itself. The categories overlap, but they make policy and ethical judgement more precise.

*Table 1. A practical spectrum of AI-assisted writing*

| Mode | Typical uses | Main governance question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assistive | Spelling and grammar; dictation clean-up; formatting; sentence prediction. | Does it remove a mechanical or access barrier while the writer controls the meaning? |
| Developmental | Brainstorming; outlining; questions; examples; feedback on a human draft. | Does it strengthen the writer's thinking instead of supplying the final answer? |
| Collaborative | Draft passages; rephrasing; tone changes; summarisation; translation. | Can the writer explain, verify and revise every material claim and expressive choice? |
| Substitutive | End-to-end generation; an undisclosed assignment; commissioned copy with no meaningful human authorship. | Does it misrepresent who did the work, invalidate an assessment or displace agreed human labour? |

*The spectrum is analytical, not legal. The same feature may be an accommodation in one context and an impermissible substitute in another.*

This distinction also fits the U.S. Copyright Office's approach. Its 2025 report states that AI used as an assistive tool does not prevent copyright protection, including for creators with disabilities, whereas prompts alone ordinarily do not provide enough human control to make the user the author of the generated expression (U.S. Copyright Office, 2025). The legal rule is jurisdiction-specific, but the underlying idea is broadly useful: human intention is not the same as human authorship unless it is expressed through meaningful control and creative choices.

## 2. Why opposition to AI writing is reasonable

### Authorship, accountability and trust

Readers often care not only about the words but about who is speaking. A personal statement is evidence of reflection; a scholarly article is a claim to expertise; a novel is valued partly as a human imaginative act. Undisclosed end-to-end generation can therefore be deceptive even when the prose is accurate. It allows a person or organisation to claim the credibility, effort or intimacy associated with authorship without performing the corresponding work.

Accountability cannot be delegated to a model. If a report defames someone, invents a source or gives unsafe advice, the system cannot retract the claim, compensate the harmed party or explain its evidence. Human sign-off is meaningful only when the signer has actually checked and understood the text. This is why many publishing and research policies allow limited assistance but keep authorship and responsibility human.

### Reliability, bias and the authority of fluent prose

Generative systems are optimised to produce plausible language, not guaranteed truth. NIST calls confident false output 'confabulation' and treats it alongside privacy, bias, homogenisation and information-integrity risks (NIST, 2024). The danger is not merely an obvious absurdity. Fluent prose can conceal a wrong inference, a missing qualification or a citation that does not exist. In a 2023 bibliographic study, 55 per cent of citations produced by GPT-3.5 and 18 per cent produced by GPT-4 were fabricated; many genuine citations also contained substantive errors (Walters & Wilder, 2023). Those rates describe older models and a particular task, but the obligation to verify remains.

Bias also operates through normalisation. Models tend to favour statistically common language and social assumptions. Disability-centred focus groups found subtle stereotypes even when outputs were not overtly offensive (Gadiraju et al., 2023). In writing, a tool may replace dialect, directness or culturally specific expression with a smooth institutional register. The result can look 'better' according to a narrow rubric while becoming less truthful to the writer.

### Learning, critical thinking and deskilling

Writing is one of the ways people learn to reason. If a student delegates idea selection, structure, evidence and phrasing at once, the submitted product reveals little about the student's ability. The immediate quality of an AI-assisted essay is therefore a poor measure of independent learning. A 2025 study of 319 knowledge workers found that greater confidence in generative AI was associated with less self-reported critical-thinking effort, while confidence in one's own task ability was associated with more (Lee et al., 2025). This is correlational evidence, not proof of inevitable cognitive decline, but it supports keeping opportunities for unaided practice and using AI as a coach, critic or source of alternatives rather than as a silent answer machine.

The educational problem is also a design problem. Assignments that reward generic, detachable prose invite automation. More authentic tasks can require local evidence, drafts, oral explanation, reflection on sources and visible revision. UNESCO consequently recommends a human-centred approach that protects agency, privacy and intellectual development rather than treating detection as the only response (UNESCO, 2023).

### Creative labour, consent and market power

Professional opposition is partly about who receives the economic benefit. Writers object when models are trained on creative work without acceptable consent or compensation, when synthetic content floods markets, or when employers use AI to lower rates while retaining the prestige of human authorship. The dispute is not solved by saying that writers also use tools: a worker choosing assistance is different from an employer imposing automation or a platform appropriating value from a body of work.

The Writers Guild of America's 2023 agreement offers a concrete governance model. It states that AI is not a writer, prevents companies from requiring writers to use it, preserves human credit and compensation, and requires disclosure when supplied material contains AI-generated content (WGAW, 2025). The details are industry-specific, but the principles – consent, disclosure, credit and bargaining power – apply more widely.

### Privacy, surveillance and unequal enforcement

Prompts may contain unpublished manuscripts, student work, client facts, health information, workplace disputes or legally privileged material. A convenient rewrite can therefore become an unauthorised disclosure. Safe use requires approved tools, data-minimisation, clear retention and training terms, and a rule against entering sensitive material when those guarantees are absent.

Attempts to police AI use can create a second injustice. In a 2023 evaluation of seven detectors, human-written TOEFL essays by non-native English writers were falsely classified as AI-generated at an average rate of 61.22 per cent; 89 of 91 essays were flagged by at least one detector (Liang et al., 2023). The tools and models have since changed, but no detector should be treated as proof. Process evidence, conversation and fair appeal are safer than automated accusation, especially for multilingual and disabled writers whose language may already be judged against a narrow norm.

## 3. The benefits of AI-assisted writing

### Faster drafting and stronger immediate output

The clearest benefit is reduced friction. In the Noy and Zhang experiment, participants produced short professional documents such as press releases, reports and emails. ChatGPT reduced average completion time by 40 per cent and increased quality by 18 per cent, with the largest benefits among lower-performing participants (Noy & Zhang, 2023). The model shifted effort away from rough drafting and towards generating ideas and editing. This suggests that AI can be valuable where the goal is a competent document under time pressure rather than a test of unaided writing.

Field evidence points in a similar direction. In a study of 5,172 customer-support agents, a generative assistant increased successful resolutions per hour by 15 per cent overall and by roughly 30 per cent among less-skilled and less-experienced workers. It also improved the comprehensibility of English-language responses, particularly for some workers writing in a second language (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). These results came from a specialised workplace system, not a general chatbot, but they show how language support can distribute expertise more quickly.

### A low-cost partner for planning, revision and feedback

Writers can ask for possible structures, counterarguments, questions a sceptical reader might raise, explanations of grammar, alternative transitions or a critique against a rubric. Used this way, AI offers immediate and repeatable feedback without embarrassment or scheduling. It can help a writer move from an unstructured idea to a plan, or from a dense draft to a clearer one, while the human remains responsible for substance.

The benefit is especially important where human feedback is scarce. AI is available outside office hours, can restate an explanation repeatedly and can adapt the level of detail. It can also create options rather than one authoritative correction. The most productive prompt is often not 'write this for me' but 'show me three structures and explain the trade-offs' or 'identify what a reader may not understand without rewriting my voice.'

### Language access and participation

Translation, vocabulary support, tone explanation and plain-language suggestions can help people participate in institutions that privilege a particular variety of written English. A second-language writer may know the subject well but spend disproportionate time on articles, idiom or institutional tone. AI can reduce that penalty. It can also help any writer convert specialist language into an accessible public explanation.

This 'levelling' effect should not be confused with a duty to sound native or standard. Assistance is empowering when it expands a writer's choices; it is assimilation when a system silently decides that dialect, accent or disability-related communication is defective. Writers need to see changes, reject them granularly and preserve an original version.

### Creativity: catalyst and convergence

AI can widen an individual's starting set of ideas. In a preregistered short-story experiment, access to GPT-4 ideas increased average ratings of novelty and usefulness, with the largest gains for less-creative writers. Yet AI-assisted stories also became more similar to one another (Doshi & Hauser, 2024). The finding captures the central creative trade-off: a shared tool can help more people cross a quality threshold while narrowing the collective range of expression.

A sensible creative practice therefore uses AI for provocation rather than closure: ask for contradictions, neglected viewpoints or deliberately unusual constraints; combine suggestions with personal observation; and delete any sentence that could have been written for anyone. Strong human voice is not a decorative layer applied after generation. It comes from the writer's selection of what matters.

## 4. Neurodivergence, disability and access

Disability changes the ethical balance because the effort required to produce text is not evenly distributed. Some barriers arise from impairment, such as pain, fatigue, decoding difficulty or limited motor control. Others are environmental: inaccessible source material, rigid deadlines, impatience with AAC, instructions full of ambiguity, or an institution that treats one style of expression as the only evidence of intelligence. AI may compensate for those failures, but it should not make them acceptable or replace existing accommodations.

In interviews with 33 disabled university students, including neurodivergent and visually impaired participants, students described ChatGPT as an assistant for writing, reading, research and self-organisation. They reported reduced time, effort and pain, as well as greater autonomy, but also robotic prose, inaccuracies, inaccessible interfaces and time lost checking results (Pierrès et al., 2025). A survey of 124 disabled students likewise found generative AI used across academic-writing tasks and recorded concerns about inaccurate answers, academic integrity and subscription costs (Zhao et al., 2025). These studies are modest and mainly self-reported, but they show why disability policy cannot treat all assistance as authorship fraud.

### Dyslexia, dysgraphia and language processing

A writer may understand a subject yet struggle to encode it quickly in conventional spelling and syntax. AI can correct a draft, explain rather than merely mark an error, suggest vocabulary, turn notes or dictation into sentences, and simplify dense source text. It can reduce the amount of working memory devoted to mechanics and make revision less punitive. The writer should still control meaning, because an apparently polished correction can introduce a claim the writer did not intend or conceal a misunderstanding that teaching needs to address.

### ADHD and executive function

The barrier may be initiation and organisation rather than ideas. Useful functions include breaking an assignment into small actions, converting an idea dump into an outline, identifying the next sentence, prioritising tasks, summarising a long thread and drafting routine administrative messages. This can bridge the gap between intention and action. It does not solve follow-through, and repeated prompting can become another source of distraction or cognitive load. Effective tools should offer short, controllable steps rather than endless suggestions.

### Autism and social communication

Some autistic people use language models to make implicit conventions explicit: what a vague instruction may mean, how a message might be interpreted, or how to rehearse a difficult conversation. This can reduce anxiety and dependence on a family member or colleague. Emerging qualitative research also reports uses as a 'translator' between neurodivergent and neurotypical perspectives (Carik et al., 2025).

The same function can automate masking. If the system always makes an autistic writer sound more indirect, emotionally demonstrative or conventionally 'warm', it places the whole burden of accommodation on that writer. Good design offers interpretation in both directions, states that several tones may be valid, and asks whether the user wants clarity, politeness, brevity or preservation of voice rather than assuming a neurotypical norm.

### Motor, speech and communication disabilities

For people who type with eye gaze, switches or limited movement, every saved action matters. Language models can expand abbreviations, predict full phrases and transform a compact input into a grammatical message. In a study with 12 AAC users, participants anticipated savings in physical and cognitive effort but insisted that suggestions remain editable, personal and removable. They also distinguished routine communication, where automation could help, from intimate communication, where generated wording might change the meaning of the act itself (Valencia et al., 2023). This is a powerful reminder that efficiency is not the only value in writing.

### Visual impairment, chronic illness, pain and fatigue

Conversational navigation, summarisation, reformatting and document description can reduce screen-reader workload. Drafting and restructuring can also conserve energy for people living with pain, fatigue or brain fog. Yet verification may impose an 'accessibility tax': a blind user may be unable to inspect a visual source the model has described, and a fatigued user may spend the energy saved on finding subtle errors. The answer is not merely to tell people to check the output. Sources and change histories must themselves be accessible, and high-stakes verification may need funded human support.

### Risks that are specific to disability access

- **Inaccessible tools.** A text-first chatbot can remain unusable with a screen reader, keyboard-only navigation, atypical speech or cognitive overload. The accessibility of the output does not excuse an inaccessible interface.

- **Privacy-for-access trade-offs.** Personalisation may require a user to disclose a diagnosis, health details, communication history or intimate examples. People should not have to barter privacy for basic participation.

- **Cost and effort.** Subscription fees, premium accessibility features and repeated prompt engineering can transfer the cost of inclusion to the disabled person.

- **Stigma and blanket bans.** Institutional rules may treat a spelling aid, AAC expansion or planning scaffold as equivalent to ghostwriting. Requiring individual permission can also force disclosure of disability. Policies need confidential access routes and clear examples.

- **Voice erasure and automated masking.** A system trained on dominant language can flatten directness, dialect, disability identity or cultural expression. 'Professionalising' and 'humanising' are not neutral operations.

The central accessibility test is agency. Assistance should increase the person's ability to communicate what they mean, not make them disappear behind a statistically average voice. It should be optional, preserve the original, show proposed changes and allow granular acceptance or rejection. It should supplement rather than replace human accommodations, disabled expertise and accessible institutional design.

## 5. A workable approach to responsible use

The debate does not require a choice between prohibition and surrender. A proportionate framework can protect the benefits while addressing the strongest objections.

1. **Keep purpose and accountability human.** The writer decides the goal, audience, claims and ethical boundaries, and remains able to explain and defend the final text.

1. **Match assistance to the real objective.** If the goal is a finished workplace document, drafting help may be appropriate. If the goal is to learn or demonstrate unaided reasoning, use AI for questions, feedback or post-draft comparison and preserve periods of independent work.

1. **Verify claims at the source.** Treat generated facts, quotations and citations as leads, not evidence. Open the underlying source, confirm that it exists and check that it supports the exact claim.

1. **Protect confidential and personal data.** Use approved systems, minimise what is entered, remove identifying details where possible, and do not upload unpublished, privileged, health or client material without an adequate contractual and security basis.

1. **Preserve voice and difference.** Ask for options and explanations instead of a single correction. Keep the original visible. State what must not change, including dialect, directness, technical meaning or disability-related communication preferences.

1. **Disclose material assistance.** Disclosure should be proportionate. Routine spelling or dictation may need no special notice where policy permits it; generated arguments, passages, translations or substantive restructuring may. The audience should not be misled about authorship or expertise.

1. **Make policies accessible and contestable.** Give concrete permitted and prohibited examples, create confidential accommodation routes, provide equitable access and training, and never use an AI detector as the sole basis for punishment.

### A tiered default for schools and workplaces

A sound policy can separate three levels. Low-risk assistive uses – such as spelling support, approved dictation, formatting and accessibility-oriented sentence prediction – are generally permitted when they do not add unverified substance. Developmental or collaborative uses – such as outlining, summarising, rewriting and generating draft passages – require human verification and may require disclosure or process records. Substitutive or high-risk uses – such as undisclosed end-to-end authorship, impersonation, use in an unaided examination, or entry of protected information into an unapproved service – are restricted.

The categories should be adjustable for context and disability. A rule should ask whether the tool changes the construct being assessed. If an exam measures subject knowledge, an approved writing aid may be compatible with fairness; if it measures unaided composition, the same aid may alter the construct. Decisions should involve disabled users and accommodation specialists rather than being imposed after procurement.

## Conclusion

AI writing is neither a trivial extension of spellcheck nor an autonomous author. It is a flexible system that can remove friction, supply language, shape thought and redistribute power. Its value depends on which of those functions is being used, by whom and under what expectations.

The strongest opposition identifies real harms: concealed authorship, unreliable claims, weakened assessment, standardised expression, loss of privacy, appropriation of creative labour and unfair enforcement. The strongest case in favour is equally real: faster drafting, more accessible feedback, language support, reduced physical and cognitive effort, and greater autonomy for people whose ideas have too often been judged through inaccessible mechanics.

The best settlement is human-led and access-preserving. Use AI to widen the routes into writing, not to make human thought, voice or responsibility optional. A good system helps a person say more clearly what they mean. A good policy ensures that the person – not the system, the institution or the market – remains visible in the result.
